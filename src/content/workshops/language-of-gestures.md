Una delle sue prime influenze fu l’attore e regista francese Jean-Louis Barrault, famoso per le sue squisite capacità di pantomima, come si vede nel film *Les Enfants du Paradis* (Children of Paradise-1945). Durante i suoi anni italiani (1948-1956) Lecoq esplorò le radici della pantomima nella Commedia tradizionale italiana e ancora più indietro, nell’antico mimo del teatro latino, che era noto per le sue scuole di pantomima. Lecoq riportò l’essenza della pantomima alle sue origini giocose, recuperando la follia gestuale degli attori della Commedia dell’Arte del Cinque e Seicento. Condusse ricerche con e senza maschere e questo chiaro linguaggio del gesto rimase al centro dell’insegnamento nella sua scuola.

  Lecoq continuò a sviluppare qualcosa chiamato *mimo d’azione*, che consiste nello studio dei movimenti della vita quotidiana, che sono alla radice dei 20 Movimenti, un insieme di esercizi tecnici per la formazione dell’attore. La precisione, l’accuratezza e il rigore specifici che accompagnano questa particolare pratica sono elementi cruciali nella formazione dell’attore-creatore del teatro fisico. L’Analisi del Movimento e la Tecnica del Movimento divennero elementi centrali di questa formazione, e un ulteriore sviluppo di questo lavoro è stato portato avanti da Norman Taylor, che ha insegnato accanto a Lecoq per quasi vent’anni e ha continuato il lavoro dopo e oltre Lecoq.

  Come le scale per un musicista, gli elementi di questa articolazione fisica, il mimo d’azione e i 20 Movimenti devono essere costantemente richiamati a chiunque si dedichi al Teatro Fisico. La pratica non finisce mai.

The Language of Gestures holds a foundational place in the pedagogy of Jacques Lecoq and the pedagogy of physical theatre in general. This workshop is a crash course in its fundamental ingredients, an opportunity for the physical theater player to connect with the deep roots of the language of the body in theatrical space.

The language of gestures combines an excited curiosity in the observation of the movements of life with the ability to reproduce them accurately; it is the art of telling stories without words. The work will be joyous as well as highly rigorous. Participants will be pushed to be precise, acutely articulate, utilizing a high level of fixed point and working with non-verbal communication. The work is highly technical, so previous experience with movement based theater is required.

*“When theater loses the body it loses its soul.”*  
-Jacques Lecoq

<!--more-->

Lecoq arrived to theater from a background in physical education and physiotherapy: an accurate and specific knowledge of the body in motion was part of his core passion. One of his first influences was the French actor and director Jean-Louis Barrault, famous for his exquisite pantomime skills, as seen in the movie *Les Enfants du Paradis* (Children of Paradise-1945). Barrault was initiated to mime by Etienne Decroux.

White-face mime, which feels rather vintage in the contemporary theatre scene, has held the roots of the work for centuries. During his Italian years (1948-1956) Lecoq explored the roots of pantomime in traditional Italian Commedia and even further back in the ancient mime of Latin theatre, which was known for its schools of pantomime. Lecoq brought the essence of pantomime back to its playful origins, reviving the gestural folly of 16th and 17th century Commedia dell’Arte performers. He researched with and without masks and this clear language of gesture remained at the core of the teaching at his school.

Lecoq continued to develop something called *action mime* which consists of the study of the movements of everyday life, which are at the roots of the 20 Movements, a set of technical exercises for the training of the actor. The specific precision, accuracy and rigor that comes with this particular practice are crucial elements in the training of the physical theatre actor-creator. Movement Analysis and Movement Technique became core elements of this training, and a further development of this work was carried on by Norman Taylor, who taught alongside Lecoq for almost twenty years and has continued the work after and beyond Lecoq.

Like scales for a musician, the elements of this physical articulation, action mime and the 20 Movements need to be constantly reminded to anyone engaged in Physical Theatre. Practice never ends.

**REQUIREMENTS**

This workshop is for theatre practitioners who have previous experiences in movement theatre and are interested in the pedagogy of Jacques Lecoq.

A fierce and playful curiosity about the human body in motion is required!
